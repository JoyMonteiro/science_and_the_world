What kind of knowledge is climate science? What makes it strong, where are its limits, and why has its meeting with society been so difficult? This course answers these questions through history, from the mechanical philosophers of the seventeenth century to today's "climate crisis".

The notes below are first drafts and will change during the semester. Start with the [course map](notes/L0-course-map.html).

## Lectures

1. **[What the mechanical philosophers promised](notes/L1-mechanical-philosophy.html)**
   *[date]* · What makes a claim about nature reliable, and why was reliability wanted?
2. **[The Romantic departure: one Europe, two sciences](notes/L2-romanticism-humboldt.html)**
   *[date]* · Do experimental and field sciences work the same way?
3. **[From climates to the climate, 1800–1979](notes/L3-bridge-climates-to-the-climate.html)**
   *[date]* · How do you do science on a single planet you cannot experiment on?
4. **[What is climate?](notes/L4-what-is-climate.html)**
   *[date]* · Whose knowledge counts, and at what scale?
5. **[The performance of science](notes/L5-performance-of-science.html)**
   *[date]* · How does climate science earn its confidence, and what can't it decide?
6. **[Alarm and restraint, 1956–1988](notes/L6-alarm-and-restraint.html)**
   *[date]* · Should scientists say what their findings mean?
7. **[Manufacturing the crisis, 1988–2024](notes/L7-manufacturing-the-crisis.html)**
   *[date]* · Who turns an "is" into an "ought"?
8. **[A crisis of what, for whom?](notes/L8-crisis-of-what-for-whom.html)**
   *[date]* · Is a claim accountable to evidence, to interest, or to both?
