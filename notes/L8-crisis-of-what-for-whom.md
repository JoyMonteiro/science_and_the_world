**The question for today:** When someone makes a claim about climate, is it accountable to *evidence*, to *interest*, or to both? How do we tell?

## Crisis is a judgement, not only a finding

The geographer Noel Castree points out that "global environmental change cannot speak for itself, it must be spoken for." Science can tell us that temperatures are rising and roughly by how much. Calling this a *crisis* adds something more:

> "Crisis is not simply a cognitive concept based on hard evidence; it's also normative."

The philosopher Max Black argued that an *is* implies a definite *ought* only under quite restrictive conditions. Between "the planet will warm 2 °C" and "this is a crisis demanding X" lie value judgements: about what matters, to whom, and compared with what. Packing those judgements into a scientific formula (for example, a mathematical definition of "climate emergency") hides them. Castree calls this "scientizing" value questions.

Castree also uses Roger Pielke Jr's "tornado politics". When a tornado is coming, everyone agrees to run, and the science tells you what to do. Most of climate politics is not like that. Even COVID-19 was not a pure tornado, because real policy choices remained.

Is this relativism? Castree says no: "De-objectifying crisis is not relativist but, instead, a mark of responsibility." Crisis claims can be misused as "rhetorical smokescreens", and they "can produce panic or hopelessness". Being clear about the values inside a crisis claim makes it *more* honest, not less true.

Mike Hulme and colleagues ask two questions of any crisis: **a crisis of what?** and **a crisis for whom?** Crisis framings "mobilize, legitimate and yet also constrain". They win attention and resources, but they can hide slow, structural causes.

## Scale: one fact, different meanings

Remember Lecture 4: climate science is most confident at the **global** scale. The crisis frame takes that global *is* and turns it into an *ought* for everyone, everywhere. But "for whom?" has a different answer at each scale. Take India:

1. **International.** In 1991 Anil Agarwal and Sunita Narain of the Centre for Science and Environment (Delhi) audited a study by the World Resources Institute, a US think tank, which had ranked countries by emissions. They argued it treated the "survival emissions" of the poor as equivalent to the "luxury emissions" of the rich. Their argument fed into the principle of "common but differentiated responsibilities", written into the 1992 UN climate convention. Per-capita emissions put responsibility on the rich North.
2. **Within the Global South.** The same per-capita argument that indicts the North can be turned against India by smaller, more exposed nations such as the small island states.
3. **National.** India's national average hides the many Indians whose lifestyles are high-carbon.
4. **The most marginalised.** For many Dalit, Adivasi and poor communities there is no new *event*-crisis. Hardship is chronic, and "even normal conditions constitute a near-crisis". A crisis is something new that can be averted; this is not.

The same number re-politicises the issue at one scale and de-politicises it at the next.

**A local case: the ice stupas of Ladakh.** Artificial glaciers ("ice stupas") store winter water for spring farming. In Phyang village a project irrigating monastery land led to protests by villagers. The hydrology was poorly known, so the local official decided on the basis of *interests*, not evidence. Neither "the global crisis" nor "local knowledge" settled it.

## Bringing the course together

In Lecture 1 we met Shapin's paradox: knowledge stripped of interest was made valuable for moral and political action. We can now see its limits. Climate science is strongly accountable to evidence at the global scale. Turning that into action at the scales where people live always involves interests and values. So we need **two separate tests** for any claim:

- **Accountable to evidence:** is it true, at the scale claimed?
- **Accountable to interest:** whose interests does this framing serve, and whose does it leave out?

These are different questions. "This study's figures are wrong" is a claim about evidence. "This study was written by Americans" is a claim about interest. Both can matter, but they must not be confused.

The historian Joshua Howe ends *Behind the Curve* with a similar lesson: "information is not the same as political change. One of the reasons that we are behind the curve on global warming is that historically we have conflated these two things." Climate change is a **wicked problem**: ill-defined, interconnected, with no clear test of what a solution looks like. It will not be "solved". As Howe puts it, "failure has many flavors, some far more palatable than others."

## What we've learned about the nature of science
- **Science's strength is that its confidence can be checked.** Climate science built its confidence about the *is* in public, testable steps. Projections made in the 1970s were borne out by later warming. Independent lines of evidence (physics, observations, the deep past) agree. The IPCC's calibrated language moved from "discernible" (1995) to "unequivocal" (2021) only as evidence accumulated (Lecture 5).
- **Science has limits as well as strengths.** Its knowledge is uncertain in different ways, and some uncertainties cannot be reduced.
- **Facts and values are different.** Science can *define* a problem without being able to *solve* it.
- **There is more than one way to relate to nature**: mastery (mechanism) or reverence (organising principles, the whole).
- **There is more than one way to know.** Experiment, observation and simulation each give different kinds of confidence.

## Questions to think about
1. Which value judgements sit between "temperatures will rise 2 °C" and "this is a crisis"? Whose values are they?
2. Was COVID-19 a "tornado"? What did "follow the science" leave out?
3. Take a recent "climate emergency" declaration by a city or ministry. What does it bring into view, and what does it hide?
4. For someone whose normal life is already precarious, does "climate crisis" describe their situation? What word would?
5. Apply the two tests to a climate claim you have seen this week.
6. Andrea Wulf ends her biography of Humboldt by citing the IPCC and calling on environmentalists to "reclaim" him as their hero. Is enlisting a historical figure for a present cause a claim about evidence, about interest, or both? (Compare what Humboldt actually meant by climate, Lecture 2.)
