title: "Lecture 6 — Alarm and restraint, 1956–1988"

**The question for today:** Should scientists say what their findings *mean*?

## The puzzle

By 1979 a major scientific assessment estimated that doubling CO₂ would warm the Earth by about 3 °C, give or take 1.5 °C. More than forty years later the IPCC's best estimate is still 3 °C; only the range has narrowed (2.5–4 °C, in 2021). Yet "climate crisis" did not become common language until the mid-2000s. Why did the science not produce the alarm on its own? This lecture and the next follow one answer: the institutions that spoke for climate science drew their authority from **refusing to say what their findings meant**.

## 1950s–60s: alarm existed, but it pointed at ice

- In 1956 a senior US Weather Bureau scientist wrote that humanity was "poised precariously on a thin climatic knife-edge". He meant a sudden new *ice age*, not greenhouse warming.
- In 1957 Revelle and Suess called fossil-fuel burning "a large scale geophysical experiment". Revelle used such warnings partly to secure research funding. He did not expect much change for decades.
- Some writers saw warming as *good*: produce "as much carbon dioxide as we can manage".
- By 1965 scientists were starting to describe climate as "a complex system, precariously balanced". What made dramatic change plausible was mainly nuclear fallout, smog, cloud-seeding and *Silent Spring*, not the CO₂ data.

## 1970s: scientists as unusual environmentalists

- Big reports (SCEP 1970, SMIC 1971) listed CO₂ as a serious risk. But as the historian Spencer Weart notes, "nobody thought of recommending actual action".
- At the 1972 Stockholm conference, developing countries reframed "environment" as a problem of *underdevelopment*. They warned that rich nations wanted to "pull the ladder up behind them". The diplomat George Kennan observed that truly global problems had "virtually no committed political constituency at all".
- Mainstream environmental groups wanted campaigns that were local, discrete and winnable. Climate fitted none of these. So climate scientists aimed their advocacy at elites (Congress, funding agencies), asking for more research. "For atmospheric scientists, the best environmental advocacy was science advocacy." This was the **forcing function of knowledge** in action.
- Some scientists did break ranks. Stephen Schneider wrote that "consensus … is a poor way to do science", at real cost to his career. The 1970s "global cooling" debate was a real scientific disagreement that was resolved, though it was later used by deniers.

## 1979: the Charney report

The US National Academy's report *Carbon Dioxide and Climate* is worth reading closely.

- Its **body** says, in neutral language, that we "may not be given a warning until the CO₂ loading is such that an appreciable climate change is inevitable."
- Its **foreword**, by Verner Suomi, adds the *ought*: "a wait-and-see policy may mean waiting until it is too late."
- The warming range, 1.5–4.5 °C, is offered as "at best informed guesses".
- It warns that models are not reliable for *regional* changes.
- Words like *crisis*, *catastrophe* or *emergency* appear nowhere.

The facts and the warning were both there. They were placed in different parts of the document, under different authors.

## 1980s: from hearings to the heat wave

- After James Hansen's 1981 warming paper made the *New York Times* front page, his promised government funding was cut.
- In 1983 the National Academy and the Environmental Protection Agency reviewed the same science in opposite tones. The Academy said, in effect, wait and monitor; the EPA warned of "potentially catastrophic" consequences. The administration used the first to dismiss the second.
- Climate became a partisan issue in the 1980s. The science had not changed; what had changed was who held power.
- At Villach (1985) and Toronto (1988), expert meetings issued stronger statements. Historians disagree about how far scientists themselves led these, and the disagreement is itself informative.
- On 23 June 1988, during a record heat wave, Hansen told the US Senate it was "time to stop waffling". Global warming became front-page news.
- Later in 1988 two UN agencies (the World Meteorological Organization and the UN Environment Programme) set up the IPCC. Unlike the invited experts at Villach and Toronto, its members were *government* representatives. Some US officials favoured that design because a lengthy process would slow action.

## The NOS lesson

Restraint here was not just a personality trait of cautious scientists. It was built into **institutions**: funding, peer norms, who sat on panels, and which document said what. The question "should scientists say what their findings mean?" has no purely scientific answer.

This is not new (Lecture 2). In the 1790s William Herschel seems to have toned down his speculations about an evolving universe as fear of radical science grew in Britain. In 1819 the surgeon William Lawrence withdrew his book on the physical basis of life under pressure from his profession. The pressures in the 1970s and 80s were different (funding, panel membership, government sponsors), but the pattern, institutions shaping what scientists say in public, is old.

## Questions to think about
1. Is publicising your findings a scientist's duty? Does the answer change if the findings are uncertain?
2. Why did climate fail as an environmental campaign in the 1970s, while local pollution succeeded?
3. Two reports on the same science reach opposite tones. Is that a failure of science?
