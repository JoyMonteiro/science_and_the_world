**The question for today:** Should scientists say what their findings *mean*?

## The puzzle

By 1979, a major scientific assessment estimated that doubling CO₂ would warm the Earth by about 3 °C, give or take 1.5 °C. More than forty years later, the IPCC's best estimate is still 3 °C, and only the range has narrowed (2.5–4 °C, in 2021). Yet "climate crisis" did not become common language until the mid-2000s. Why did the science not produce the alarm on its own?

This lecture and the next follow one answer to this question: the institutions that spoke for climate science drew their authority from **refusing to say what their findings meant**. We trace this decade by decade, from the 1950s to the founding of the IPCC in 1988, and pause along the way to read one document, the 1979 Charney report, closely.

## 1950s–60s: alarm existed, but it pointed at ice

- In 1956 a senior US Weather Bureau scientist wrote that humanity was "poised precariously on a thin climatic knife-edge". He meant a sudden new *ice age*, not greenhouse warming.
- In 1957 Revelle and Suess called fossil-fuel burning "a large scale geophysical experiment". Revelle used such warnings partly to secure research funding, and did not himself expect much change for decades.
- Some writers saw warming as *good*, and recommended producing "as much carbon dioxide as we can manage".
- By 1965 scientists were beginning to describe climate as "a complex system, precariously balanced". What made dramatic change seem plausible was mainly nuclear fallout, smog, cloud-seeding and *Silent Spring*, rather than the CO₂ data.

## 1970s: scientists as unusual environmentalists

- Large reports (SCEP 1970, SMIC 1971) listed CO₂ as a serious risk. But, as the historian Spencer Weart notes, "nobody thought of recommending actual action".
- At the 1972 Stockholm conference, developing countries reframed "environment" as a problem of *underdevelopment*, and warned that rich nations wanted to "pull the ladder up behind them". The diplomat George Kennan observed that truly global problems had "virtually no committed political constituency at all".
- Mainstream environmental groups wanted campaigns that were local, discrete and winnable, and climate fitted none of these. Climate scientists therefore aimed their advocacy at elites — Congress and the funding agencies — and asked for more research. "For atmospheric scientists, the best environmental advocacy was science advocacy." This is the **forcing function of knowledge** (Lecture 5) in action.
- Some scientists did break ranks. Stephen Schneider wrote that "consensus … is a poor way to do science", at real cost to his career. The 1970s "global cooling" debate, we note, was a real scientific disagreement which was resolved, though deniers later made use of it.

## 1979: the Charney report

The US National Academy's report *Carbon Dioxide and Climate* repays close reading:

- Its **body** says, in neutral language, that we "may not be given a warning until the CO₂ loading is such that an appreciable climate change is inevitable."
- Its **foreword**, by Verner Suomi, adds the *ought*: "a wait-and-see policy may mean waiting until it is too late."
- The warming range, 1.5–4.5 °C, is offered as "at best informed guesses".
- It warns that models are not reliable for *regional* changes.
- Words such as *crisis*, *catastrophe* or *emergency* appear nowhere in it.

Thus, both the facts and the warning were present in the report — but they were placed in different parts of the document, under different authors.

## 1980s: from hearings to the heat wave

- After James Hansen's 1981 warming paper made the front page of the *New York Times*, his promised government funding was cut.
- In 1983 the National Academy and the Environmental Protection Agency reviewed the same science in opposite tones. The Academy said, in effect, wait and monitor; the EPA warned of "potentially catastrophic" consequences. The administration used the first to dismiss the second.
- Climate became a partisan issue in the 1980s. The science had not changed; what had changed was who held power.
- At Villach (1985) and Toronto (1988), expert meetings issued stronger statements. Historians disagree about how far scientists themselves led these, and that disagreement is itself informative.
- On 23 June 1988, during a record heat wave, Hansen told the US Senate that it was "time to stop waffling", and global warming became front-page news.
- Later in 1988 two UN agencies, the World Meteorological Organization and the UN Environment Programme, set up the IPCC. Unlike the invited experts at Villach and Toronto, its members were *government* representatives. Some US officials favoured this design because they expected a lengthy process to slow action.

## Nature of science: what climate adds

**The textbook statements.** "Science is a human endeavor" (NSTA 2020, p. 4). The NSTA explains that the questions asked, the observations made and the conclusions drawn are influenced by "the social and cultural context of the researcher, and the observer's experiences and expectations" (p. 3).

**What the climate case adds.** The list places social influence mainly in the individual researcher — their context, experiences and expectations. The history of 1956–1988 places it in **institutions**: in funding, in peer norms, in who sat on panels and in which document said what. The restraint we have seen here was not simply a personality trait of cautious scientists, and the question "should scientists say what their findings mean?" therefore has no purely scientific answer. We note, too, that the influence acted less on what was *found* than on what was *said* in public. The Charney report contains both the facts and the warning, placed in different sections under different authors. "The funding sources of scientists", which Allchin and colleagues quote as something every citizen should ask about (2014, p. 463), is the most visible part of this, but not the whole of it.

Nor is this new (Lecture 2). In the 1790s William Herschel seems to have toned down his speculations about an evolving universe as fear of radical science grew in Britain, and in 1819 the surgeon William Lawrence withdrew his book on the physical basis of life under pressure from his profession. The pressures of the 1970s and 1980s were different — funding, panel membership, government sponsors — but the underlying pattern, in which institutions shape what scientists say in public, is an old one.

**For the science classroom.** If social influence is taught only as personal bias, students have two options when they meet it: deny it, or conclude that the science is corrupt. Clough and Herman argue that suspicion of climate science after "climategate" reflected exactly this kind of naïve view, which overlooks how individual biases in "private science" are reduced when "methods, data, and ideas are scrutinized by the wider scientific community" (2017, p. 18). The history in this lecture offers a third option: to describe the institutions through which science speaks, and to ask what each one encourages and discourages. The Charney exercise below is a model for a classroom activity of this kind, and it requires no climate science beyond what is in the report itself.

## In class
Compare the Charney report's foreword (pp. vii–viii) with its summary (p. 2). Circle every *addressee* (who is being spoken to), every *modal* (may, must, should) and every *metaphor*. What does the foreword add that the body does not?

## Questions to think about
1. Is publicising your findings a scientist's duty? Does the answer change if the findings are uncertain?
2. Why did climate fail as an environmental campaign in the 1970s, while local pollution succeeded?
3. Two reports on the same science reach opposite tones. Is that a failure of science?
