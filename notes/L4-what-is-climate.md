**The question for today:** Whose knowledge of climate counts, and at what scale?

## Does climate exist?

Weather exists in an obvious sense: you can get wet. Climate is different. Nobody can directly sense it, and nobody can directly measure "the climate of Paris" or "the temperature of the planet". Climate is an **organising principle for weather**: a way of making sense of the variability we experience. Mike Hulme puts it like this: "Climate is weather which has been cultured, interpreted and acted on by the imagination, through story-telling and using material technologies."

So climate is *both* a physical and a cultural idea. Science is one of the cultures that makes sense of weather, and it is a very powerful one. But it is not the only one.

## One word, two meanings

- **Physical meaning:** measurable quantities (temperature, rainfall) averaged over a reference period. The idea of a climatic "normal" was introduced by the geographer Eduard Brückner in 1890; his was a 35-year mean. In the 1930s the International Meteorological Organization recommended 30 years, and that convention is still used. This meaning detached climate from the sensory experience of weather and from particular places. The historian Matthias Heymann calls global mean temperature "an artificial parameter divested of all spatial information and inaccessible to the senses", and it has become the lead number of climate science.
- **Cultural meaning:** what the weather of a place means to the people who live there, for work, health, identity, morality and memory. "Mild for February" is a climate judgement you make without any statistics.

Treating these as two separate things is, Hulme argues, a peculiarly Western habit. In many languages one word covers weather, air, spirit and right conduct together.

Climate ideas have also been used for many purposes. Some were benign, such as planning agriculture. Some were not: in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, "climatic determinism" was used to claim that people from temperate climates were naturally superior, and so to justify empire.

## Ways of knowing climate

- **Personal:** your senses, memory, stories, TV.
- **Communal:** shared memory, especially of extremes ("the year the river flooded").
- **Scientific:** systematic measurement and statistics (descriptive) plus computer models built on physical understanding (predictive).
- **Consensual:** knowledge agreed on through bodies such as the IPCC.

Hulme distinguishes first-hand knowledge (experience), second-hand (others' accounts) and third-hand (scientific reconstruction). *Climate change* is third-hand knowledge for everyone, including climate scientists.

## The view from nowhere and the view from somewhere

The global climate is, in Hulme's phrase, "a view of climate for everyone but from nowhere". It belongs to no particular place, and that is its strength. But the IPCC's picture is also a view *from somewhere*: from the institutions, places and practices of science.

## Authority depends on scale

Here is the key point for the rest of the course. Climate science is *most* confident at the **global** scale. The physics of how the whole planet warms (its energy budget, its thermodynamics) is well understood. Science is *least* confident at **regional and local** scales, because those depend on changes in atmospheric circulation, which are much harder to predict. And people live locally.

This was recognised from the start. The 1979 Charney report, the first major US assessment of CO₂ and climate, said: "we do not consider the existing models to be at all reliable in their predictions of regional climatic changes."

Confidence also depends on *what* is being predicted, not only where. Climate scientists distinguish two kinds of change:
- **Thermodynamic changes** follow fairly directly from a warmer world, and they are robust even locally. Hot extremes have become hotter and more frequent over most land regions. Warmer air holds about 7 % more water vapour per degree, so the heaviest downpours get heavier.
- **Dynamic changes** depend on how the circulation of the atmosphere shifts, and these remain uncertain. An example is how much monsoon rain a particular state will receive.

So "local" does not mean "unknowable". It means the answer depends on the question.

## Science moves down the scale: event attribution

Since 2004 a new branch of climate science has asked whether climate change made a *particular* heatwave, flood or drought more likely or more intense. It compares the world as it is with a simulated world without human emissions. The climate scientist Friederike Otto argues that attribution "turns climate change from an abstract threat in the future to being a felt reality today". Some of its most useful results are cases where climate change is *not* to blame, which shows that the local causes (water use, poor planning) can be fixed. The philosopher Greg Lusk replies that attribution looks backwards, and that using it to assign blame is "a political act".

Either way, scientific authority is now reaching down towards the scale where people live. Lecture 8 asks who uses that authority, and for what.

So two kinds of authority sit in different places:
- **Scientific (predictive, descriptive) authority** is strong globally and thin locally.
- **Cultural (normative) authority**, what matters and what to do, is strong locally and thin globally.

Neither covers the scale at which politics actually happens. That is one reason people disagree about climate change, and the disagreement is not simply ignorance.

## Two guardrails
1. **This is not relativism.** Saying climate has many meanings does not make the physical meaning false. Global warming is real and largely human-caused.
2. **"Local" is not automatically just.** Local and traditional knowledge can also carry exclusions, for example of caste or gender.

## Questions to think about
1. List one piece of your own climate knowledge for each of: first-hand, second-hand, third-hand.
2. Why might climate change be "such a big deal" in Sweden (glaciers, skiing, polar bears) for different reasons than in Maharashtra?
3. If scientific confidence is highest at the scale where nobody lives, who should decide what to do locally?
4. Is a farmer's knowledge of when the monsoon "should" arrive a kind of climate knowledge? How does it compare with a 30-year average?
5. An attribution study finds that a heatwave in your city was made three times more likely by climate change. What does that tell the city government to do, and what does it leave open?
