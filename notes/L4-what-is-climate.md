**The question for today:** Whose knowledge of climate counts, and at what scale?

## Does climate exist?

Weather exists in an obvious sense: you can get wet. Climate is different. Nobody can directly sense it, and nobody can directly measure "the climate of Paris" or "the temperature of the planet". Climate is better thought of as an **organising principle for weather** — a way of making sense of the variability we experience from day to day and year to year. Mike Hulme puts it like this: "Climate is weather which has been cultured, interpreted and acted on by the imagination, through story-telling and using material technologies."

Climate is thus *both* a physical and a cultural idea. Science is one of the cultures that makes sense of weather, and a very powerful one, but it is not the only one. In this lecture we first look at the two meanings of the word and the different ways in which climate is known. We then come to the idea that the rest of the course leans on — that the authority of climate knowledge depends on scale — and look at how a new branch of climate science, event attribution, is pushing scientific authority down towards the local scale.

## One word, two meanings

- **Physical meaning:** measurable quantities (temperature, rainfall) averaged over a reference period. The idea of a climatic "normal" was introduced by the geographer Eduard Brückner in 1890, whose normal was a 35-year mean. In the 1930s the International Meteorological Organization recommended 30 years, a convention that is still in use. This meaning detached climate both from the sensory experience of weather and from particular places. The historian Matthias Heymann calls global mean temperature "an artificial parameter divested of all spatial information and inaccessible to the senses", and yet it has become the lead number of climate science.
- **Cultural meaning:** what the weather of a place means to the people who live there — for their work, health, identity, morality and memory. "Mild for February" is a climate judgement that you make without any statistics at all.

Treating these as two separate things is, Hulme argues, a peculiarly Western habit. In many languages, a single word covers weather, air, spirit and right conduct together.

Ideas about climate have also been put to many uses. Some were benign, such as planning agriculture. Some were not: in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, "climatic determinism" was used to claim that people from temperate climates were naturally superior, and thereby to justify empire.

## Ways of knowing climate

- **Personal:** your senses, memory, stories, television.
- **Communal:** shared memory, especially of extremes ("the year the river flooded").
- **Scientific:** systematic measurement and statistics (descriptive), together with computer models built on physical understanding (predictive).
- **Consensual:** knowledge agreed upon through bodies such as the IPCC.

Hulme distinguishes first-hand knowledge (experience), second-hand knowledge (other people's accounts) and third-hand knowledge (scientific reconstruction). It is worth noting that *climate change* is third-hand knowledge for everyone, including climate scientists.

## The view from nowhere and the view from somewhere

The global climate is, in Hulme's phrase, "a view of climate for everyone but from nowhere". It belongs to no particular place, and that is its strength. But the IPCC's picture is also a view *from somewhere* — from the institutions, places and practices of science.

## Authority depends on scale

We now come to an idea that the rest of the course relies on. Climate science is *most* confident at the **global** scale, where the physics of how the whole planet warms — its energy budget, its thermodynamics — is well understood. It is *least* confident at **regional and local** scales, since changes there depend on changes in the atmospheric circulation, which are much harder to predict. And people, of course, live locally.

This was recognised from the very start. The 1979 Charney report, the first major US assessment of CO₂ and climate, said: "we do not consider the existing models to be at all reliable in their predictions of regional climatic changes."

Confidence also depends on *what* is being predicted, and not only on where. Climate scientists distinguish between two kinds of change:
- **Thermodynamic changes** follow fairly directly from a warmer world, and they are robust even locally. Hot extremes have become hotter and more frequent over most land regions. Warmer air can hold about 7 % more water vapour per degree Celsius of warming, and so the heaviest downpours get heavier.
- **Dynamic changes** depend on how the circulation of the atmosphere shifts, and these remain uncertain. How much monsoon rain a particular state will receive is one example.

Thus, "local" does not mean "unknowable". It means that the answer depends on the question being asked.

## Science moves down the scale: event attribution

Since 2004, a new branch of climate science has asked whether climate change made a *particular* heatwave, flood or drought more likely or more intense. It does so by comparing the world as it is with a simulated world without human emissions. The climate scientist Friederike Otto argues that attribution "turns climate change from an abstract threat in the future to being a felt reality today". Some of its most useful results, in fact, are cases where climate change is *not* to blame, since these show that the local causes — water use or poor planning, for example — can be fixed. The philosopher Greg Lusk replies that attribution looks backwards, and that using it to assign blame is "a political act".

Either way, scientific authority is now reaching down towards the scale at which people live. Who uses that authority, and for what, is a question we take up in Lecture 8.

We are therefore left with two kinds of authority, which sit in different places:
- **Scientific (predictive, descriptive) authority** is strong globally and thin locally.
- **Cultural (normative) authority** — what matters and what to do — is strong locally and thin globally.

Neither of these covers the scale at which politics actually happens. This is one of the reasons why people disagree about climate change, and it is why that disagreement cannot simply be put down to ignorance.

## Two guardrails
1. **This is not relativism.** Saying that climate has many meanings does not make the physical meaning false. Global warming is real and largely human-caused.
2. **"Local" is not automatically just.** Local and traditional knowledge can also carry exclusions, of caste or gender, for example.

## Nature of science: what climate adds

**The textbook statements.** "Science addresses questions about the natural and material world" and "Science is a way of knowing", distinguished in the NSTA statement "from other ways of knowing" (NSTA 2020, pp. 2, 4). The NSTA also says that science "must be the sole focus of instruction in science classes to the exclusion of all nonscientific or pseudoscientific methods, explanations, generalizations, and products" (p. 2), while acknowledging that contributions to science "have been made by people the world over" (p. 3).

**What the climate case adds.** The list draws a line between science and other ways of knowing. In climate, that line runs through the concept itself. The physical meaning of climate rests on conventions (a 30-year "normal"), and the cultural meaning — what the weather of a place means to those who live there — is not a rival theory of the atmosphere but an answer to a different question. Allchin and colleagues criticise consensus lists for mixing claims about what knowledge is with claims about what the world is (2014, p. 463), and climate shows why this matters: before asking whether a claim is scientific, we need to ask what *kind* of object climate is. The lecture's account of scale-dependent authority suggests a more useful question than "science or not?": which kind of knowledge is authoritative for *which* question, at *which* scale? Global energy balance and a farmer's sense of when the monsoon "should" arrive are not competing for the same ground.

**For the science classroom.** Students arrive with first- and second-hand knowledge of climate: the year the river flooded, the summers that seem hotter than they used to. Read literally, the NSTA's exclusion clause leaves a teacher with nothing to say about this knowledge except that it is not science. An alternative is to treat it as evidence about a different scale and a different question, and to use the two guardrails above: this is not relativism, and local knowledge is not automatically just. Clough and Herman argue that NOS teaching should help students "navigate their own and others' socio-cultural biases" (2017, pp. 21–22), which is difficult to do if those students' own experience of climate is ruled out of the classroom.

## Questions to think about
1. List one piece of your own climate knowledge for each of: first-hand, second-hand, third-hand.
2. Why might climate change be "such a big deal" in Sweden (glaciers, skiing, polar bears) for different reasons than in Maharashtra?
3. If scientific confidence is highest at the scale where nobody lives, who should decide what to do locally?
4. Is a farmer's knowledge of when the monsoon "should" arrive a kind of climate knowledge? How does it compare with a 30-year average?
5. An attribution study finds that a heatwave in your city was made three times more likely by climate change. What does that tell the city government to do, and what does it leave open?
