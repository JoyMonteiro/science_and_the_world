title: "Course map — Science and the World: climate"

**The course in one question:** What kind of knowledge is climate science, what makes it strong, where are its limits — and why has its encounter with society been so difficult?

This course does not treat the nature of science as an abstract topic. We will instead approach it through history, and in particular through the history of one science, climate science, whose relationship with society has been unusually fraught. Each lecture is organised around a single question:

| # | Lecture | The question |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | What the mechanical philosophers promised | What makes a claim about nature reliable, and why was reliability wanted? |
| 2 | The Romantic departure: one Europe, two sciences | Do experimental and field sciences work the same way? |
| 3 | From climates to *the* climate, 1800–1979 | How do you do science on a single planet you cannot experiment on? |
| 4 | What is climate? | Whose knowledge counts, and at what scale? |
| 5 | The performance of science | How does climate science earn its confidence, and what can't it decide? |
| 6 | Alarm and restraint, 1956–1988 | Should scientists say what their findings *mean*? |
| 7 | Manufacturing the crisis, 1988–2024 | Who turns an "is" into an "ought"? |
| 8 | A crisis of what, for whom? | Is a claim accountable to evidence, to interest, or to both? |

**Three movements.** Lectures 1 and 2 look at two ways of doing science — taking nature apart (mechanism) and seeing it whole (Humboldt). Lecture 2 also compares two Romantic sciences that grew up in the same Europe, one British and one German, to show how a culture shapes a science as well as its dealings with patrons, the public and the state. Lectures 3–5 follow climate science as it grew out of the second of these traditions, and ask what it knows and how it knows it. Lectures 6–8 then ask how a well-established scientific finding came to be called a "crisis", and what that framing does.

## Four ideas that keep coming back
1. **Shapin's paradox (L1 → L5 → L8).** Science became useful for settling political disputes *because* it claimed to be free of interests. Climate science is asked to play exactly this role, and much of the pressure on it follows from that.
2. **Scale (L2 → L4 → L8).** Climate science is most confident about the planet as a whole and least confident about particular places — which is, of course, where people live and make decisions. We note, however, that some local changes, such as hotter extremes and heavier downpours, are nonetheless well understood.
3. **Is and ought (L5 → L8).** Science can tell us what is happening. Deciding what to do about it also requires values. Much of the history in this course is about who took that step from "is" to "ought", when, and how.
4. **Strength *and* limits.** Nothing in this course puts the warming itself in doubt. It is real and human-caused, and climate science's confidence in this has been tested repeatedly and has held. The question the course asks is a different one: what does that confidence license, at which scale and for whom?

## The two tests (where we end up)
By the end of the course, I would like you to be able to ask two separate questions of any claim about climate:
- **Accountable to evidence:** is it true, at the scale claimed?
- **Accountable to interest:** whose interests does this framing serve, and whose does it leave out?

## How to use the notes
The notes for each lecture give its argument, key terms, readings with page numbers and some questions to think about. Please read the **core** reading before the lecture. Readings marked "by section" come from books whose PDFs lack printed page numbers.
