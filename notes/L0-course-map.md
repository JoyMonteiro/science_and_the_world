**The course in one question:** What kind of knowledge is climate science? What makes it strong, where are its limits, and why has its meeting with society been so difficult?

We answer this through history, not as an abstract topic. Each lecture has one question at its centre.

| # | Lecture | The question |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | What the mechanical philosophers promised | What makes a claim about nature reliable, and why was reliability wanted? |
| 2 | The Romantic departure: one Europe, two sciences | Do experimental and field sciences work the same way? |
| 3 | From climates to *the* climate, 1800–1979 | How do you do science on a single planet you cannot experiment on? |
| 4 | What is climate? | Whose knowledge counts, and at what scale? |
| 5 | The performance of science | How does climate science earn its confidence, and what can't it decide? |
| 6 | Alarm and restraint, 1956–1988 | Should scientists say what their findings *mean*? |
| 7 | Manufacturing the crisis, 1988–2024 | Who turns an "is" into an "ought"? |
| 8 | A crisis of what, for whom? | Is a claim accountable to evidence, to interest, or to both? |

**Three movements.** Lectures 1–2 cover two ways of doing science: taking nature apart (mechanism) and seeing it whole (Humboldt). Lecture 2 also compares two Romantic sciences from the same Europe, British and German, to show how a culture shapes a science and its dealings with patrons, the public and the state. Lectures 3–5 cover how climate science grew from the second of these, what it knows and how. Lectures 6–8 cover how a well-established finding became a "crisis", and what that frame does.

## Four ideas that keep coming back
1. **Shapin's paradox (L1 → L5 → L8).** Science became useful for settling political disputes *because* it claimed to be free of interests. Climate science is asked to play exactly this role.
2. **Scale (L2 → L4 → L8).** Climate science is most confident about the whole planet, and least confident about particular places, where people live and decide. Some local changes (hotter extremes, heavier downpours) are nonetheless well understood.
3. **Is and ought (L5 → L8).** Science can say what is happening. What to do about it also needs values. The interesting history lies in who made that step, when and how.
4. **Strength *and* limits.** Nothing in this course says the warming is in doubt. It is real and human-caused, and climate science's confidence in it has been tested and has held. The course asks a different question: what does that confidence license, at which scale, and for whom?

## The two tests (where we end up)
For any claim about climate, ask separately:
- **Accountable to evidence:** is it true, at the scale claimed?
- **Accountable to interest:** whose interests does this framing serve, and whose does it leave out?
