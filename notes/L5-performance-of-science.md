**The question for today:** How does climate science earn its confidence, and what can it not decide?

## "Science isn't doing the job we expect"

We often expect science to deliver the truth and politics then to act on it. On climate change, this has not happened. Mike Hulme argues that many disputes labelled "scientific" are really disputes about values, about risk or about the proper role of science in policy. Disagreement is normal in science — science, in fact, "thrives" on it. The difficulty arises when we ask science to settle questions that it cannot settle.

Before asking what science *cannot* decide, however, we look at how climate science has tested what it claims. We then distinguish three kinds of uncertainty, look at the IPCC as a body that produces consensus, and compare three models of how science meets policy. We end with the choice between two kinds of error that every scientist has to make, and what that choice implies.

## How climate science earns its confidence

Four checks matter here.

**1. Did the predictions come true?** The strongest test of a model is a prediction made *before* the data existed. In 2020, Zeke Hausfather and colleagues took 17 projections of global warming published between 1970 and 2001 and compared them with what happened afterwards. Ten of the 17 matched the observed warming directly. Most of the misses had assumed the wrong future levels of greenhouse gases and other influences ("forcings") — that is, they made a wrong guess about human behaviour, and not about the physics. After allowing for this, 14 of the 17 matched. The authors found "no evidence that the climate models … have systematically overestimated or underestimated warming".

**2. Do independent lines of evidence agree?** Consider *climate sensitivity*, the warming that follows a doubling of CO₂. Three largely independent kinds of evidence bear on it: the physics of feedbacks (clouds, water vapour, ice), the warming already observed and the climates of the deep past. None of them is conclusive on its own, and indeed there is "no such thing as a purely observational estimate of climate sensitivity". The 1979 range of 1.5–4.5 °C stood for forty years. Only by combining all three lines of evidence did a 2020 assessment narrow it, to about 2.6–3.9 °C.

**3. What goes on inside a model?** Some processes, such as clouds and convection, are too small or too poorly understood to be calculated directly. Models represent them with simplified rules that have adjustable settings, or parameters, and choosing those settings is called **tuning**. Modellers say openly that "the decisions on what to tune … undoubtedly involve value judgments". In one case, a new model warmed far too much (about 7 °C per doubling of CO₂), and its builders deliberately tuned it down to about 3 °C to match the warming of the twentieth century. This is why a model's match to the *past* is weak evidence in its favour: "the desired result is known in advance". Predictions of the future (check 1) and independent lines of evidence (check 2) are much stronger tests.

**4. How sure, exactly?** The IPCC uses a calibrated vocabulary: *likely* means a probability of at least 66 %, *very likely* at least 90 %, *extremely likely* at least 95 % and *virtually certain* at least 99 %. Here is how its headline statement on the human cause of warming has changed:

| Report | Headline on human influence |
|---|---|
| 1990 | Warming is "broadly consistent with predictions of climate models", but unequivocal detection "is not likely for a decade or more" |
| 1995 | "The balance of evidence suggests a discernible human influence on global climate" |
| 2001 | Most of the warming of the last 50 years is "likely" due to greenhouse gases |
| 2007 | "Very likely" |
| 2013 | "Extremely likely" that human influence has been the dominant cause |
| 2021 | "It is unequivocal that human influence has warmed the atmosphere, ocean and land" |

Confidence rose step by step over three decades as the evidence came in. Please keep this **confidence ladder** in mind; we will need it again in Lecture 7.

We note that values do appear inside this process, in tuning and in deciding what counts as "likely". Nevertheless, the results have proved reliable. Values inside science do not, by themselves, make its claims unreliable. What matters is whether the claims survive tests which could have failed.

## Three kinds of uncertainty

1. **Epistemic:** we do not fully understand the system. More data and better theory can, in principle, reduce this kind of uncertainty — although some model errors cannot be fixed simply by running more simulations.
2. **Aleatoric / chaotic:** the climate system is partly unpredictable by its very nature. At best, we can obtain probabilities.
3. **Scenario (deep) uncertainty:** future emissions depend on human choices. Here there are no probabilities, only scenarios.

Each of these needs a different response, and treating all uncertainty as a case of "we just need more research" mixes them up. Climate modellers use a similar three-way split of their own — *model* uncertainty, *internal variability* (the system's own chaos) and *scenario* uncertainty — and which of these dominates depends on how far ahead you look.

## Post-normal science

Funtowicz and Ravetz described situations where "facts are uncertain, values in dispute, stakes high and decisions urgent". In such cases, the normal model of science, in which experts quietly solve puzzles, is not enough. Post-normal science asks scientists to be open about what is *unknown*, to reflect on the values embedded in their choices and to widen the circle of who counts as an expert.

## Consensus and the IPCC

The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (founded 1988) is a **boundary organisation**: it sits between science and politics, and is "policy relevant, not policy prescriptive". Government delegates approve its summaries line by line.

- **Strength:** consensus built through an open, well-governed process is probably "the least worst" way of stating what is known.
- **Weakness:** consensus can *under-state* uncertainty. In 2007, for example, the IPCC narrowed its sea-level projection by leaving out ice-sheet processes that it could not yet quantify.
- **Who is at the table?** Experts are nominated by governments, and the panel has historically been weighted towards the Global North and towards natural scientists.

## Three models of how science meets policy

1. **Decisionist:** politicians choose the goals, and scientists advise on the means.
2. **Technocratic:** "sound science" determines policy, so that truth speaks to power. Science then becomes the battleground for every political fight.
3. **Co-production:** goals and means are worked out together, with both science and public values in the open.

The historian Joshua Howe calls the technocratic hope the **"forcing function of knowledge"** — the belief that better science would *force* political action. As one scientist put it in the 1970s, "A worldwide scientific consensus … will be the forcing function for the difficult political decisions." How that hope played out is the subject of the next two lectures.

Performance has long roots (Lecture 2). Davy's lectures at the Royal Institution packed London's streets with carriages, and Humboldt made pictures of the whole Earth because, as he said, "the world likes to see". Science has always had to be shown as well as done. What is new with the IPCC is that the performance is one of *agreement*, staged by an institution, rather than of a discovery staged by a person.

A distinction due to Robert Proctor is useful here. **Objectivity** concerns whether science's claims are reliable; **neutrality** concerns whether science takes a stand. These are not the same thing, and a science can be objective without being neutral.

## Models as prophets

Climate models give us a glimpse of possible futures; they do not show us *the* future. In interpreting them, scientists must choose between two kinds of error: claiming an effect that is not there (Type I, a false alarm) or missing one that is (Type II). Climate science, like most science, guards hardest against Type I errors — it would rather miss a real effect than raise a false alarm. Historians of climate assessment call this "erring on the side of least drama". This caution has a cost. When the stakes are high, missing a real danger can be worse than a false alarm, and some climate scientists argue that the balance should shift. Which error to fear more is a *value judgement*, not a scientific finding, and yet it is made inside science. Lectures 6 and 7 show what followed from it.

Hulme's conclusion is that "We should not hide behind science when difficult ethical choices are called for."

Recall Lecture 1. The mechanical philosophers promised that disinterested knowledge could settle disputes. Thus, what we have seen in this lecture are the limits of that promise.

## Nature of science: what climate adds

**The textbook statements.** "Scientific knowledge is simultaneously reliable and subject to change." The NSTA explains that "because scientific knowledge is partly the result of inference, creativity, and subjectivity, it is subject to change", and that "some subjectivity is unavoidable and often beneficial" (NSTA 2020, p. 2).

**What the climate case adds.** The list says that knowledge is *both* reliable and tentative, but not how much confidence a particular claim deserves. Climate science has had to answer that question in public, and this lecture shows how: predictions checked against later data, independent lines of evidence and a calibrated vocabulary in which "likely" and "virtually certain" mean different things. The confidence ladder from 1990 to 2021 is tentativeness and reliability made quantitative. Without it, the statement "science is tentative" is easily read as "science is unsure, so wait" — which is how it was used against climate science (Lecture 7). Clough and Herman name this directly as an NOS issue: the mistaken belief "that science, when well done, leads to absolute certainty and that public action is not warranted until such certainty is established" (2017, p. 17). Against it they set the view "that overwhelming confidence without absolute certainty is the best that can be achieved with complex phenomena" (pp. 18–19).

The subjectivity statement is sharpened in the same way. Tuning involves value judgements, and yet the projections held. What made the difference is the step from what Clough and Herman call *private* science, where an individual's biases operate, to *public* science, where methods and data are scrutinised by others and those biases are "significantly diminished" (2017, p. 18). The IPCC is an institution for exactly that step, with its own weaknesses (who sits at the table, the risk of understating uncertainty).

**For the science classroom.** Most students, and most adults, will never evaluate climate data themselves. What they will do is decide whom to believe. Allchin and colleagues put "assessing the trustworthiness and reliability of scientific claims in a public setting" at the centre of NOS teaching (2014, p. 463), and Clough and Herman ask that students learn to "assess which experts are credible and why" (2017, pp. 21–22). Teaching tentativeness on its own is therefore not enough. It should be paired with an account of how confidence is graded and earned, for which the IPCC's ladder and Hausfather's test of old projections are ready-made classroom material.

1. Why is a model's match to warming *after* its projection was published stronger evidence than its match to the warming that came before?
2. For each kind of uncertainty, give a climate example and say what would reduce it, if anything.
3. Should the IPCC recommend policies? What would it gain and lose?
4. A scientist must choose between risking a false alarm and missing a real danger. Who should make that choice?
