**The question for today:** How does climate science earn its confidence, and what can it not decide?

## "Science isn't doing the job we expect"

We often expect science to deliver truth and then let politics act on it. On climate change that hasn't happened. Mike Hulme argues that many disputes labelled "scientific" are really disputes about values, about risk, or about the proper role of science in policy. Disagreement is normal in science; it "thrives" on it. The problem is when we ask science to settle questions it cannot settle.

## How climate science earns its confidence

Before asking what science *cannot* decide, look at how climate science has tested what it claims. Four checks matter.

**1. Did the predictions come true?** The strongest test of a model is a prediction made *before* the data existed. In 2020 Zeke Hausfather and colleagues took 17 projections of global warming published between 1970 and 2001 and compared them with what happened afterwards. Ten of the 17 matched the observed warming directly. Most of the misses had assumed the wrong future levels of greenhouse gases and other influences ("forcings"). That is a wrong guess about human behaviour, not a fault in the physics. After allowing for it, 14 of the 17 matched. The authors found "no evidence that the climate models … have systematically overestimated or underestimated warming".

**2. Do independent lines of evidence agree?** Take *climate sensitivity*, the warming from doubled CO₂. Three largely independent kinds of evidence bear on it: the physics of feedbacks (clouds, water vapour, ice), the warming already observed, and the climates of the deep past. None of them is conclusive alone, and there is "no such thing as a purely observational estimate of climate sensitivity". The 1979 range of 1.5–4.5 °C stood for forty years. Only by combining all three lines did a 2020 assessment narrow it, to about 2.6–3.9 °C.

**3. What goes on inside a model?** Some processes are too small or too poorly understood to calculate directly; clouds and convection are examples. Models represent them with simplified rules that have adjustable settings (parameters). Choosing those settings is called **tuning**. Modellers say openly that "the decisions on what to tune … undoubtedly involve value judgments". In one case a new model warmed far too much (about 7 °C per doubling of CO₂), and its builders deliberately tuned it down to about 3 °C to match the warming of the twentieth century. This is why matching the *past* is weak evidence for a model: "the desired result is known in advance". Predictions of the future (check 1) and independent lines of evidence (check 2) are stronger tests.

**4. How sure, exactly?** The IPCC uses a calibrated vocabulary. *Likely* means a probability of at least 66 %, *very likely* at least 90 %, *extremely likely* at least 95 %, *virtually certain* at least 99 %. Here is how its headline statement on the human cause of warming changed:

| Report | Headline on human influence |
|---|---|
| 1990 | Warming is "broadly consistent with predictions of climate models", but unequivocal detection "is not likely for a decade or more" |
| 1995 | "The balance of evidence suggests a discernible human influence on global climate" |
| 2001 | Most of the warming of the last 50 years is "likely" due to greenhouse gases |
| 2007 | "Very likely" |
| 2013 | "Extremely likely" that human influence has been the dominant cause |
| 2021 | "It is unequivocal that human influence has warmed the atmosphere, ocean and land" |

Confidence rose step by step over three decades, as the evidence came in. Keep this **confidence ladder** in mind for Lecture 7.

Values appear inside this process, in tuning and in choosing what counts as "likely". Yet the results have proved reliable. Values inside science do not by themselves make its claims unreliable. What matters is whether the claims survive tests that could have failed.

## Three kinds of uncertainty

1. **Epistemic:** we don't fully understand the system. More data and better theory can reduce this, in principle. Some model errors, though, cannot be fixed just by running more simulations.
2. **Aleatoric / chaotic:** the climate system is partly unpredictable by nature. At best we get probabilities.
3. **Scenario (deep) uncertainty:** future emissions depend on human choices. There are no probabilities here, only scenarios.

Each needs a different response. Treating all uncertainty as "we just need more research" mixes them up. Climate modellers use a similar three-way split of their own: *model* uncertainty, *internal variability* (the system's own chaos) and *scenario* uncertainty. Which one dominates depends on how far ahead you look.

## Post-normal science

Funtowicz and Ravetz described situations where "facts are uncertain, values in dispute, stakes high and decisions urgent". In such cases the normal model of science (experts quietly solving puzzles) is not enough. Post-normal science asks scientists to be open about what is *unknown*, to reflect on the values in their choices, and to widen who counts as an expert.

## Consensus and the IPCC

The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (founded 1988) is a **boundary organisation**. It sits between science and politics, and it is "policy relevant, not policy prescriptive". Government delegates approve its summaries line by line.

- **Strength:** consensus built through an open, well-governed process is probably "the least worst" way to state what is known.
- **Weakness:** consensus can *under-state* uncertainty. In 2007 the IPCC narrowed its sea-level projection by leaving out ice-sheet processes it couldn't yet quantify.
- **Who is at the table?** Experts are nominated by governments, and the panel has historically been weighted toward the Global North and toward natural scientists.

## Three models of how science meets policy

1. **Decisionist:** politicians choose the goals, scientists advise on the means.
2. **Technocratic:** "sound science" determines policy, so truth speaks to power. Science then becomes the battleground for every political fight.
3. **Co-production:** goals and means are worked out together, with science and public values both in the open.

The historian Joshua Howe calls the technocratic hope the **"forcing function of knowledge"**: the belief that better science would *force* political action. As one scientist put it in the 1970s, "A worldwide scientific consensus … will be the forcing function for the difficult political decisions." The next two lectures show how that hope played out.

Performance has long roots (Lecture 2). Davy's lectures at the Royal Institution packed London's streets with carriages, and Humboldt made pictures of the whole Earth because, as he said, "the world likes to see". Science has always had to be shown as well as done. What is new with the IPCC is that the performance is of *agreement*, staged by an institution, not of a discovery staged by a person.

A useful distinction from Robert Proctor: **objectivity** is whether science's claims are reliable. **Neutrality** is whether science takes a stand. They are not the same. A science can be objective and still not neutral.

## Models as prophets

Climate models give a glimpse of possible futures; they don't show *the* future. Scientists must choose between two kinds of error: claiming an effect that isn't there (Type I, a false alarm), or missing one that is (Type II). Climate science, like most science, guards hardest against Type I errors: it would rather miss a real effect than raise a false alarm. Historians of climate assessment call this "erring on the side of least drama". It has a cost. When the stakes are high, missing a real danger can be worse than a false alarm, and some climate scientists argue the balance should shift. Choosing which error to fear more is a *value judgement*, not a scientific finding. It is made inside science, and Lectures 6 and 7 show what followed from it.

Hulme's conclusion: "We should not hide behind science when difficult ethical choices are called for."

Recall Lecture 1. The mechanical philosophers' promise was that disinterested knowledge could settle disputes. Here we see the limits of that promise.
