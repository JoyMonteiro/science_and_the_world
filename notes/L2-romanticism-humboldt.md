**The question for today:** Do the experimental sciences (physics, chemistry) and the field sciences (ecology, biogeography, climate) share the same "nature of science"? Why, or why not?

**A second question runs underneath it:** if the culture a science grows up in shapes that science, what does that do to the science's relationship with society?

## The reaction

By the late eighteenth century the mechanical, Enlightenment picture had critics. If nature is a machine and humans are part of nature, are humans machines too? Does perfecting nature dehumanise us? The Romantic reaction stressed emotion, wonder and reverence over cold calculation. It looked to invisible "forces" and "powers" (electricity, magnetism, chemical affinity, life) rather than corpuscles bumping into each other. Its slogan, roughly: *man in nature, not above it*.

It is tempting to treat "Romantic science" as a speculative detour that hard-headed empirical science later corrected. Historians now argue that this picture was itself a polemic, written by mid-nineteenth-century professional scientists. Most Romantic natural philosophers wanted to *complete* mechanism and natural history, not throw them away. Whole disciplines were created or reshaped in this period: biology, the historical earth sciences, electrochemistry, plant geography.

## Organising principles

Two problems pushed science beyond mechanism:

- **Kant's problem.** Observations alone don't make a science; you need organising principles. By that standard Kant doubted that chemistry was a proper science at all.
- **Linnaeus's problem.** Exploration was bringing back so many new species that classification became endless. *Catalogue to what end?* Goethe's answer was to look for the underlying type, the "archetype", which you cannot see in any single plant. You see it only through long immersion in many.

Romantic writers described science as "a personal interaction with nature, not an autopsy". Doing science increases knowledge *and* changes the observer.

## Turning the hierarchy upside down

From Newton onward, a hierarchy of sciences was taken for granted. Mathematical physics was at the top, and everything else was measured against it. Romantic philosophy turned this around. Hegel put the sciences of inorganic matter at the *bottom* and moved upward through chemistry and electricity to the life sciences and then psychology. Schelling argued that nature "can only be understood as a system, a whole". Any approach that ignored this "can solve only local puzzles". In the nineteenth century a new kind of science, a "physics of the world" that drew on all the others, gained great prestige.

## One Europe, two Romantic sciences

Here is a natural experiment from history. Between about 1790 and 1830, Britain and the German lands shared the same Enlightenment inheritance: the same instruments, the same journals, the same letters crossing the Channel even in wartime. Both had a Romantic movement. Yet they produced recognisably different sciences.

### Britain: wonder, experiment and spectacle

The biographer Richard Holmes calls the British version "Romantic science". Its heroes were experimenters and voyagers.

- **William Herschel**, a German-born musician in Bath, ground his own telescope mirrors (in moulds of horse dung, in the family drawing room) and found the planet Uranus in 1781. He then argued that faint stars were far away, not small, and that the heavens were changing and evolving, with a history of their own (Holmes: "more like some enormous living organism"). Looking deep into space meant looking back in time. His sister **Caroline Herschel** swept the sky alongside him, found her own comets, and became the first woman in Britain to be paid a royal salary for scientific work.
- **Humphry Davy**, a self-taught chemist from Cornwall, experimented on his own body. At the radical Pneumatic Institution in Bristol he breathed carbon monoxide (it nearly killed him) and nitrous oxide, recorded his pulse as he went, and asked poets (Coleridge, Southey) to describe their sensations: subjective reports, even poems, treated as data. Moved to London's Royal Institution, he became a star lecturer whose crowds made Albemarle Street London's first one-way street. His wire-gauze safety lamp for coal miners (1815–16) came from first studying the gas, firedamp (methane), and only then designing the lamp.
- **Joseph Banks**, President of the Royal Society for 42 years, sat at the centre of it all: patron, broker of royal money, organiser of voyages and of Kew's plant collections across the empire.

Notice what is Romantic here and what is not. The *method* stayed solidly experimental and Baconian. What changed was the **persona** (the genius, the voyager, the celebrity lecturer), the **aims** (wonder, hope, "practical power ... to lessen the miseries" of humanity, in Davy's words), the **objects** (invisible powers, deep space, life itself) and the **audience** (paying crowds, fashionable society, women readers). German *Naturphilosophie* was viewed with suspicion as foreign speculation.

### The German lands: the whole, measured

In Jena in the 1790s, among Goethe, Schiller and the young philosopher Schelling, the Romantic turn went deeper: into how knowing works. Goethe held that in a machine the parts make the whole, while in an organism "the whole shaped the parts". The observer's feelings were part of knowing nature, not noise to be removed.

**Alexander von Humboldt** (1769–1859) took this into the field. His travels in South America (1799–1804) produced a science he called *physique du monde*, terrestrial physics:

- **Measure, don't just collect.** He carried dozens of instruments and measured temperature, pressure, altitude, magnetism, even the blueness of the sky, everywhere he went. He moved from naming plants to asking *where* and *in what conditions* they grow.
- **Order emerges from interacting forces.** In his words, "the general equilibrium, which reigns amongst disturbances and apparent turmoil, is the result of an infinity of mechanical forces and chemical attractions balancing each other out." No single mechanism explains the pattern; the whole does.
- **Seeing the whole at once.** His *Tableau physique des Andes* (1807) shows a mountain in cross-section, with vegetation zones, crops, geology and measurements in a single picture.
- **Aesthetics counted.** Humboldt thought the feeling a landscape produces was itself a kind of evidence about it.

Be careful not to make Humboldt more "Romantic" than he was. Specialist historians stress that he measured obsessively and never let feeling replace reason. His popular biographer, Andrea Wulf, leans the other way. Reading the two side by side is itself an exercise in how history of science is written.

### Side by side

| | Britain | German lands (Humboldt) |
|---|---|---|
| Typical science | chemistry, astronomy, exploration | plant geography, terrestrial physics, climate |
| Typical method | experiment, often on oneself; instruments built by hand | measurement everywhere; comparison across continents; maps and pictures of the whole |
| What "Romantic" changed | persona, aims, audience | how knowing works: the whole before the parts, the observer inside the picture |
| Who paid | the Royal Society and the crown; private subscribers who wanted useful results; the Navy | family money; royal pensions; a Tsar who wanted mining intelligence |
| How the public met it | paid lectures and spectacle | free public lectures (half the audience women); books at every price |

Look at the first two rows. **Britain's Romantic science was mostly experimental, and Humboldt's was a field science.** Today's question, whether experimental and field sciences share one nature of science, was already being answered in two different ways, by two cultures, from the same starting point.

## Climate as an organising principle

In 1817 Humboldt drew the first **isotherms**, lines joining places with the same *average* temperature. They showed that climate does not simply follow latitude, as the ancient Greeks had assumed. Averaging became a way of finding law-like order beneath the "turmoil" of daily weather, with local peculiarities treated as deviations from the mean.

Notice what has happened. Climate, the organising principle, is not something you can see at any one moment. It appears only when you measure over large areas and long times and then average. Climate science is born in this second, "holistic" lineage, not the mechanical one, and not in the British laboratory either.

A caution: **Humboldt's own definition of climate was not an average.** For him climate meant all the changes in the atmosphere "that noticeably affect our organs": temperature, humidity, wind, the purity of the air, even the cheerfulness of the sky and its effect on human mood. Later scientists kept his tool (the average) and dropped his definition.

## What difference does the outlook make to science and society?

The two traditions met the public, patrons and the state differently. Five lessons carry through the rest of the course.

1. **Who pays shapes what gets built.** George III paid about £4,000 for Herschel's forty-foot telescope, a national showpiece that was a worse instrument for deep sky work than his smaller twenty-foot. The Royal Institution's subscribers wanted agricultural chemistry and tanning; Davy also gave them electrochemistry and two new elements (potassium and sodium). Humboldt's Russian expedition was paid for by a Tsar interested in diamonds and gold, on condition that he said nothing about serfdom. *Ask of any science: who paid, and what did they want?*
2. **Wonder builds a public, and the public judges.** Davy's lectures and the balloon craze ("chemistry plus showmanship equalled crowds plus wonder plus money", in Holmes's phrase) made science popular. They also invited ridicule: cartoons of laughing gas, "pneumatic revellers", charges of quackery. Science that sells itself on usefulness is judged on delivery. Davy's lamp was hailed as a lifesaver; his later scheme to protect the Navy's copper hulls worked chemically but made barnacles grow faster, and the press mocked him. Humboldt instead gave free lectures and sold a picture of the world, not a device.
3. **Politics polices science.** In 1790s Britain, fear of revolution made materialist ideas dangerous: a mob burned the chemist Priestley's library, and Herschel quietly toned down his speculations. In 1819 the surgeon William Lawrence argued that life needed no "vital principle" beyond the body's organisation. He was accused of atheism and forced to withdraw his book. Mary Shelley's *Frankenstein* (1818) grew partly out of this same debate. Humboldt, a lifelong critic of slavery and of colonial damage to the land, still kept quiet in Russia and lived on a king's pension. (Lectures 6 and 7 return to scientists who restrain what they say.)
4. **"Disinterested" knowledge always has a customer.** Banks's botany doubled as a map of imperial opportunity; in 1799 he drafted a plan for Britain to take the West African coast "by conquest or by Treaty". Humboldt, "above national interests", handed President Jefferson Spanish colonial data on the disputed Texas border. Both believed in a republic of science above politics, and both served politics. This is Shapin's paradox from Lecture 1, now with a national accent.
5. **Both stories end in professionalisation.** In 1831 young British reformers founded the British Association for the Advancement of Science, copying a German travelling meeting that Humboldt had chaired in Berlin. In 1833–34 William Whewell coined the word "scientist". Specialised disciplines brought standards, peer review and a wider membership, but they lost the generalist. Humboldt, who tried to hold all of nature in one book (*Cosmos*), was soon half-forgotten. Climate science later had to rebuild a science of the whole out of specialised parts (Lecture 3).

**A guardrail.** None of this means that science is "just culture". The outlook shaped *which questions were asked, how scientists presented themselves, and to whom they spoke*. It did not decide which answers survived. Goethe, the great Romantic, held a theory of the Earth's rocks that Humboldt himself abandoned after seeing volcanoes. Herschel's Uranus was recognised slowly, through weeks of checking, not in a flash of genius. Davy's lamp worked because methane behaves as it does. Different cultures produced different sciences; evidence still disciplined each of them.

## Questions to think about
1. What is the difference between studying a plant in a botanical garden and studying it where it grows? What can each tell you that the other can't?
2. Would you rather study *differences* (Linnaeus) or *similarities* (Humboldt)? What does each make visible?
3. Can a feeling, such as awe at a landscape, ever count as evidence? Under what conditions? Davy wrote poems about breathing nitrous oxide and treated them as data. Was he right to?
4. Climate cannot be observed at any instant. Is it "real" in the same way a thunderstorm is?
5. Britain and the German lands started from the same science and ended with different ones. Name one thing in today's Indian science (who funds it, how it reaches the public, what it is expected to deliver) that might shape what it studies.
6. Davy refused to patent the safety lamp, "for humanity". His engineer friend warned that without a patent nobody could control the quality of lamps sent underground. Who was right?
