**The question for today:** Do the experimental sciences (physics, chemistry) and the field sciences (ecology, biogeography, climate) share the same "nature of science"? Why, or why not?

**A second question runs underneath it:** if the culture a science grows up in shapes that science, what does this do to the science's relationship with society?

We begin with the Romantic reaction against the mechanical picture of Lecture 1 and the two problems that pushed science beyond mechanism. We then compare the Romantic sciences that grew up in Britain and in the German lands, look at how climate emerged as an organising principle in the second of these, and end with five lessons about how a science's outlook shapes its dealings with society.

## The reaction

By the late eighteenth century the mechanical, Enlightenment picture of nature had its critics. If nature is a machine and humans are part of nature, are humans machines too? Does perfecting nature dehumanise us? The Romantic reaction stressed emotion, wonder and reverence over cold calculation, and it looked to invisible "forces" and "powers" — electricity, magnetism, chemical affinity, life — rather than to corpuscles bumping into each other. Its slogan was, roughly, *man in nature, not above it*.

It is tempting to treat "Romantic science" as a speculative detour that hard-headed empirical science later corrected. Historians now argue that this picture was itself a polemic, written by the professional scientists of the mid-nineteenth century. In fact, most Romantic natural philosophers wanted to *complete* mechanism and natural history, not throw them away, and whole disciplines were created or reshaped in this period: biology, the historical earth sciences, electrochemistry and plant geography among them.

## Organising principles

Two problems pushed science beyond mechanism:

- **Kant's problem.** Observations alone do not make a science; you also need organising principles. By that standard, Kant doubted that chemistry was a proper science at all.
- **Linnaeus's problem.** Exploration was bringing back so many new species that classification had become endless. *Catalogue to what end?* Goethe's answer was to look for the underlying type, the "archetype", which cannot be seen in any single plant and becomes visible only through long immersion in many.

Romantic writers described science as "a personal interaction with nature, not an autopsy". Doing science, on this view, increases knowledge *and* changes the observer.

## Turning the hierarchy upside down

From Newton onward, a hierarchy of the sciences was taken for granted, with mathematical physics at the top and everything else measured against it. Romantic philosophy turned this around. Hegel put the sciences of inorganic matter at the *bottom* and moved upward through chemistry and electricity to the life sciences and then to psychology. Schelling argued that nature "can only be understood as a system, a whole", and that any approach which ignored this "can solve only local puzzles". In the nineteenth century, a new kind of science — a "physics of the world" which drew on all the others — gained great prestige.

## One Europe, two Romantic sciences

History affords us something close to a natural experiment here. Between about 1790 and 1830, Britain and the German lands shared the same Enlightenment inheritance: the same instruments, the same journals and the same letters crossing the Channel even in wartime. Both had a Romantic movement. Yet they produced recognisably different sciences.

### Britain: wonder, experiment and spectacle

The biographer Richard Holmes calls the British version "Romantic science". Its heroes were experimenters and voyagers.

- **William Herschel**, a German-born musician in Bath, ground his own telescope mirrors (in moulds of horse dung, in the family drawing room) and found the planet Uranus in 1781. He went on to argue that faint stars were far away rather than small, and that the heavens were changing and evolving, with a history of their own — "more like some enormous living organism", in Holmes's words. Looking deep into space meant looking back in time. His sister **Caroline Herschel** swept the sky alongside him, found her own comets and became the first woman in Britain to be paid a royal salary for scientific work.
- **Humphry Davy**, a self-taught chemist from Cornwall, experimented on his own body. At the radical Pneumatic Institution in Bristol he breathed carbon monoxide (which nearly killed him) and nitrous oxide, recording his pulse as he went, and asked poets such as Coleridge and Southey to describe their sensations — subjective reports, even poems, treated as data. After moving to London's Royal Institution he became a star lecturer, whose crowds made Albemarle Street London's first one-way street. His wire-gauze safety lamp for coal miners (1815–16) came from first studying the gas itself, firedamp (methane), and only then designing the lamp.
- **Joseph Banks**, President of the Royal Society for 42 years, sat at the centre of it all — patron, broker of royal money and organiser of voyages and of Kew's plant collections across the empire.

We should be careful about what is Romantic here and what is not. The *method* remained solidly experimental and Baconian. What changed was the **persona** (the genius, the voyager, the celebrity lecturer), the **aims** (wonder, hope and "practical power ... to lessen the miseries" of humanity, in Davy's words), the **objects** (invisible powers, deep space, life itself) and the **audience** (paying crowds, fashionable society, women readers). German *Naturphilosophie*, on the other hand, was viewed with suspicion as foreign speculation.

### The German lands: the whole, measured

In Jena in the 1790s, among Goethe, Schiller and the young philosopher Schelling, the Romantic turn went deeper — into how knowing itself works. Goethe held that in a machine the parts make the whole, while in an organism "the whole shaped the parts". The observer's feelings were part of knowing nature, and not noise to be removed.

**Alexander von Humboldt** (1769–1859) took this into the field. His travels in South America (1799–1804) produced a science he called *physique du monde*, or terrestrial physics:

- **Measure, don't just collect.** He carried dozens of instruments and measured temperature, pressure, altitude, magnetism and even the blueness of the sky everywhere he went. In doing so he moved from naming plants to asking *where*, and *under what conditions*, they grow.
- **Order emerges from interacting forces.** In his words, "the general equilibrium, which reigns amongst disturbances and apparent turmoil, is the result of an infinity of mechanical forces and chemical attractions balancing each other out." No single mechanism explains the pattern; it is the whole that does.
- **Seeing the whole at once.** His *Tableau physique des Andes* (1807) shows a mountain in cross-section, with vegetation zones, crops, geology and measurements in a single picture.
- **Aesthetics counted.** Humboldt thought that the feeling a landscape produces was itself a kind of evidence about it.

It is worth resisting the temptation to make Humboldt more "Romantic" than he was. Specialist historians stress that he measured obsessively and never let feeling replace reason. His popular biographer, Andrea Wulf, leans the other way. Reading the two side by side is itself an exercise in how the history of science gets written.

### Side by side

| | Britain | German lands (Humboldt) |
|---|---|---|
| Typical science | chemistry, astronomy, exploration | plant geography, terrestrial physics, climate |
| Typical method | experiment, often on oneself; instruments built by hand | measurement everywhere; comparison across continents; maps and pictures of the whole |
| What "Romantic" changed | persona, aims, audience | how knowing works: the whole before the parts, the observer inside the picture |
| Who paid | the Royal Society and the crown; private subscribers who wanted useful results; the Navy | family money; royal pensions; a Tsar who wanted mining intelligence |
| How the public met it | paid lectures and spectacle | free public lectures (half the audience women); books at every price |

Look at the first two rows. **Britain's Romantic science was mostly experimental, while Humboldt's was a field science.** Thus, today's question — whether experimental and field sciences share one nature of science — was already being answered in two different ways by two cultures that started from the same place.

## Climate as an organising principle

In 1817 Humboldt drew the first **isotherms**, lines joining places that have the same *average* temperature. They showed that climate does not simply follow latitude, as the ancient Greeks had assumed. Averaging became a way of finding law-like order beneath the "turmoil" of daily weather, with local peculiarities treated as deviations from the mean.

We note that climate, the organising principle, is not something you can see at any one moment. It appears only when you measure over large areas and long times and then average. Climate science is therefore born in this second, "holistic" lineage — not in the mechanical one, and not in the British laboratory either.

A caution is in order, however. **Humboldt's own definition of climate was not an average.** For him, climate meant all the changes in the atmosphere "that noticeably affect our organs": temperature, humidity, wind, the purity of the air and even the cheerfulness of the sky and its effect on human mood. Later scientists kept his tool (the average) and dropped his definition.

## What difference does the outlook make to science and society?

The two traditions met the public, their patrons and the state in different ways. Five lessons from this carry through the rest of the course:

1. **Who pays shapes what gets built.** George III paid about £4,000 for Herschel's forty-foot telescope, a national showpiece which was a worse instrument for deep-sky work than his smaller twenty-foot. The Royal Institution's subscribers wanted agricultural chemistry and tanning; Davy also gave them electrochemistry and two new elements, potassium and sodium. Humboldt's Russian expedition was paid for by a Tsar interested in diamonds and gold, on condition that Humboldt said nothing about serfdom. *Of any science, ask: who paid, and what did they want?*
2. **Wonder builds a public, and the public judges.** Davy's lectures and the balloon craze ("chemistry plus showmanship equalled crowds plus wonder plus money", in Holmes's phrase) made science popular. They also invited ridicule — cartoons of laughing gas, "pneumatic revellers" and charges of quackery. A science that sells itself on usefulness is judged on whether it delivers. Davy's lamp was hailed as a lifesaver, but his later scheme to protect the Navy's copper hulls, which worked chemically, made barnacles grow faster, and the press mocked him. Humboldt, by contrast, gave free lectures and sold a picture of the world rather than a device.
3. **Politics polices science.** In 1790s Britain, fear of revolution made materialist ideas dangerous: a mob burned the chemist Priestley's library, and Herschel quietly toned down his speculations. In 1819 the surgeon William Lawrence argued that life needed no "vital principle" beyond the body's organisation; he was accused of atheism and forced to withdraw his book. Mary Shelley's *Frankenstein* (1818) grew partly out of this same debate. Humboldt, a lifelong critic of slavery and of colonial damage to the land, nevertheless kept quiet in Russia and lived on a king's pension. (We return to scientists who restrain what they say in Lectures 6 and 7.)
4. **"Disinterested" knowledge always has a customer.** Banks's botany doubled as a map of imperial opportunity; in 1799 he drafted a plan for Britain to take the West African coast "by conquest or by Treaty". Humboldt, supposedly "above national interests", handed President Jefferson Spanish colonial data on the disputed Texas border. Both believed in a republic of science above politics, and both served politics. This is Shapin's paradox from Lecture 1, now with a national accent.
5. **Both stories end in professionalisation.** In 1831 young British reformers founded the British Association for the Advancement of Science, copying a German travelling meeting that Humboldt had chaired in Berlin. In 1833–34 William Whewell coined the word "scientist". Specialised disciplines brought standards, peer review and a wider membership, but at the cost of the generalist. Humboldt, who had tried to hold all of nature in one book (*Cosmos*), was soon half-forgotten — and, as we will see in Lecture 3, climate science later had to rebuild a science of the whole out of specialised parts.

**A guardrail.** None of this means that science is "just culture". The outlook of a culture shaped *which questions were asked, how scientists presented themselves and to whom they spoke*. It did not decide which answers survived. Goethe, the great Romantic, held a theory of the Earth's rocks that Humboldt himself abandoned after seeing volcanoes. Herschel's Uranus was recognised slowly, through weeks of checking, and not in a flash of genius. And Davy's lamp worked because methane behaves the way it does, whatever the culture of the person who designed the lamp. Thus, while different cultures produced different sciences, evidence continued to discipline each of them.

## Nature of science: what climate adds

**The textbook statements.** "Scientific investigations use a variety of methods" (NSTA 2020, p. 3), which the NSTA elsewhere lists as "descriptive, correlational, and experimental designs" (p. 1). Also: science "affects society and cultures, and it is affected by the society and culture within which it occurs" (p. 3).

**What the climate case adds.** The list presents methods as items on a menu, all equally available. The history in this lecture shows that methods came with a *rank*: from Newton onward, the experimental and mathematical sciences sat at the top, and Romantic philosophy had to argue for the dignity of a science of the whole. Humboldt's method — measuring everywhere, comparing across continents and averaging — produced an object, climate, that never appears in any single observation. This is a different relation between evidence and conclusion from Davy's, where the gas either does or does not explode. Climate science inherits the lower-ranked method. Seen in this light, the teachers' belief that climate science must rest on controlled experiments (Lecture 1; Clough & Herman 2017, p. 16) looks like the old hierarchy still at work.

The statement that culture affects science is also true but incomplete. Britain and the German lands show *where* culture acts — on the questions asked, the persona of the scientist, the audience and the patron — and the guardrail above shows where it stops: evidence decided which answers survived. A student who has learned only that "science is culturally embedded" has no way to tell these two apart. The inventory Allchin and colleagues' teachers worked from treats culture and funding as separate, specific features to be looked for in a case (2014, p. 477), and the five lessons above are examples of each.

**For the science classroom.** Clough and Herman find that understanding the legitimacy of "observational as opposed to controlled experiments, triangulation of disparate data sources, modeling" is tied to accepting climate science (2017, p. 18). One way to teach this is to set an experimental and a field investigation of the same topic side by side — a plant grown in a pot under controlled light, and the same plant surveyed along a hillside, as Humboldt did — and ask students what each can and cannot show. The comparison of Britain and Germany offers a second lesson for teachers: the claim "science is affected by culture" should come with a guardrail, or students may conclude that it is *only* culture.

## Questions to think about
1. What is the difference between studying a plant in a botanical garden and studying it where it grows? What can each tell you that the other can't?
2. Would you rather study *differences* (Linnaeus) or *similarities* (Humboldt)? What does each make visible?
3. Can a feeling, such as awe at a landscape, ever count as evidence? Under what conditions? Davy wrote poems about breathing nitrous oxide and treated them as data. Was he right to?
4. Climate cannot be observed at any instant. Is it "real" in the same way a thunderstorm is?
5. Britain and the German lands started from the same science and ended with different ones. Name one thing in today's Indian science (who funds it, how it reaches the public, what it is expected to deliver) that might shape what it studies.
6. Davy refused to patent the safety lamp, "for humanity". His engineer friend warned that without a patent nobody could control the quality of lamps sent underground. Who was right?
