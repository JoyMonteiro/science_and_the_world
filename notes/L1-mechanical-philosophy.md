**The question for today:** What makes a claim about nature *reliable*, and why did people in the seventeenth century want reliable knowledge so badly?

## Why start here?

This course ends with climate change and the "climate crisis". It starts four centuries earlier because many of our expectations of science were set then: that it gives one universal answer, that it is free of human interests, and that it should therefore settle what we ought to do. Those expectations come under great strain when they meet a science like climate science. To see why, we first need to see where they came from.

## Before: taming nature

For educated Europeans of the Renaissance (c. 1450–1620), nature was something to tame. Wild forests, bogs and mountains were unproductive and threatening. The ideal was the drained field, the mine and above all the designed garden, nature arranged to serve human ideas of order and beauty. The study of nature was mostly descriptive and useful (collecting, cataloguing, illustrating) rather than a search for laws. The cosmos was a web of elements, humours, planetary influences and correspondences, in which humans participated.

## The natural machine

The "mechanical philosophers" of the seventeenth century (Descartes, Gassendi, Boyle and others) replaced this with a new picture:

- **Matter is passive.** In the older Aristotelian view, things had natures and purposes: a stone falls *because* it seeks its natural place. In the new view, matter has no purposes. It is only matter in motion, and things happen because particles push on particles.
- **Nature is like a clock.** A clock does complicated things, but nothing in it "wants" anything. Its behaviour follows from the arrangement of its parts. This meant dropping the old distinction between natural things and artificial ones. If nature is a machine, then experiments with machines (like Boyle's air pump) can tell us about nature.
- **Primary and secondary qualities.** Size, shape and motion are really in things. Colour, taste and warmth are effects in *us*.
- **Proximate and ultimate causes.** Science explains how the gears turn. Why the clock exists at all (the watchmaker, God) lies outside it. Paradoxically, an inert, intelligible nature was felt to *strengthen* religion: a machine needs a maker.

## How was it known?

The new philosophers said they read "the Book of Nature" rather than the books of the ancients, and trusted their own experience over authority. In practice experience had to be *organised*:

- Francis Bacon wanted a vast register of carefully collected facts, a "natural history", before anyone theorised. *Don't generalise; ask what and how first, why later.*
- Boyle made experimental facts public by performing them before witnesses and writing them up in such detail that readers became "virtual witnesses".
- Not everyone's testimony counted equally. The judgements of "cultured" gentlemen were trusted over those of the "vulgar".
- Boyle thought facts could be firm while their causes stayed only probable. Others (Descartes, Hobbes, and in a different way Newton) wanted certainty about causes.

## What was it for?

Europe had just been torn apart by religious wars. Part of the appeal of the new philosophy was that it promised agreement: questions settled by evidence anyone could see, not by dogma. Knowledge of nature also promised power (Bacon: "knowledge and power meet in one"), evidence of God's design, and mastery over nature.

## The paradox we will keep returning to

The historian Steven Shapin ends his book with a paradox. Natural knowledge was made valuable for moral and political action precisely by being stripped of moral and political interests. Because science claimed to be *disinterested*, people could appeal to it to settle disputes about what to do.

Keep this in mind. It is exactly what is asked of climate science today, and it is also why climate science comes under such pressure.

## Questions to think about
1. Can an experiment on an artificial device (an air pump, a particle accelerator, a climate model) tell us about nature? What would have to be true for it to do so?
2. Is gravity a *correct explanation* or a *good model*? Does the difference matter?
3. Whose testimony do you trust about nature, and why? Has the answer changed since Boyle?
4. If a court used a "disinterested" AI to sentence prisoners, would that make the sentences more just? What does your answer say about Shapin's paradox?
