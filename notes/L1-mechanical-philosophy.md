**The question for today:** What makes a claim about nature *reliable*, and why did people in seventeenth-century Europe want reliable knowledge so badly?

## Why start here?

This course ends with climate change and the "climate crisis", but it begins some four centuries earlier. Many of the expectations we bring to science were set in the seventeenth century: that it gives one universal answer, that it is free of human interests, and that it should therefore be able to settle what we ought to do. These expectations come under considerable strain when they meet a science like climate science, and to understand why, we first need to see where they came from.

We begin with the view of nature that the mechanical philosophers inherited from the Renaissance. We then look at the picture they replaced it with, how they claimed to know nature and what they wanted that knowledge for. We close with a paradox that will follow us through the rest of the course.

## Before: taming nature

For educated Europeans of the Renaissance (c. 1450–1620), nature was something to be tamed. Wild forests, bogs and mountains were seen as unproductive and threatening, and the ideal was the drained field, the mine and, above all, the designed garden — nature arranged to serve human ideas of order and beauty. The study of nature was mostly descriptive and useful (collecting, cataloguing, illustrating) rather than a search for laws. The cosmos itself was a web of elements, humours, planetary influences and correspondences, in which humans participated.

## The natural machine

The "mechanical philosophers" of the seventeenth century — Descartes, Gassendi and Boyle among them — replaced this with a new picture:

- **Matter is passive.** In the older Aristotelian view, things had natures and purposes: a stone falls *because* it seeks its natural place. In the new view, matter has no purposes at all. It is only matter in motion, and things happen because particles push on other particles.
- **Nature is like a clock.** A clock does complicated things, but nothing in it "wants" anything; its behaviour follows from the arrangement of its parts. This meant dropping the old distinction between natural things and artificial ones. If nature is a machine, then experiments with machines — Boyle's air pump, for example — can tell us about nature.
- **Primary and secondary qualities.** Size, shape and motion are really in things. Colour, taste and warmth are effects produced in *us*.
- **Proximate and ultimate causes.** Science explains how the gears turn. Why the clock exists at all (the watchmaker, God) lies outside it. Interestingly, an inert and intelligible nature was felt to *strengthen* religion, since a machine needs a maker.

## How was it known?

The new philosophers claimed to read "the Book of Nature" rather than the books of the ancients, and to trust their own experience over authority. In practice, however, experience had to be *organised*:

- Francis Bacon wanted a vast register of carefully collected facts — a "natural history" — before anyone theorised. *Don't generalise; ask what and how first, and why later.*
- Boyle made experimental facts public by performing them before witnesses and by writing them up in such detail that readers could become "virtual witnesses".
- Not everyone's testimony counted equally. The judgements of "cultured" gentlemen were trusted over those of the "vulgar".
- Boyle thought that facts could be firm even while their causes remained only probable. Others — Descartes, Hobbes and, in a different way, Newton — wanted certainty about causes.

## What was it for?

Europe had just been torn apart by religious wars. Part of the appeal of the new philosophy was that it promised agreement: questions would be settled by evidence that anyone could see, and not by dogma. Knowledge of nature also promised power (Bacon: "knowledge and power meet in one"), evidence of God's design and mastery over nature.

## The paradox we will keep returning to

The historian Steven Shapin ends his book with a paradox. Natural knowledge was made valuable for moral and political action precisely by being stripped of moral and political interests. Because science claimed to be *disinterested*, people could appeal to it to settle disputes about what to do.

We will meet this paradox again and again. It describes quite precisely what is asked of climate science today, and it also explains much of the pressure that climate science has come under.

## Looking ahead: the same experiment, a century later

In 1768 the painter Joseph Wright of Derby showed Boyle's air pump being demonstrated by candlelight to a family. A bird is dying in the evacuated glass, a child hides her eyes, and the lecturer looks straight out at us. The experiment is the same one Boyle performed, but the mood is no longer one of calm reason — it is closer to wonder and unease. What happened to science when its culture changed in this way? We will take up this question in Lecture 2.

## Questions to think about
1. Can an experiment on an artificial device (an air pump, a particle accelerator, a climate model) tell us about nature? What would have to be true for it to do so?
2. Is gravity a *correct explanation* or a *good model*? Does the difference matter?
3. Whose testimony do you trust about nature, and why? Has the answer changed since Boyle?
4. If a court used a "disinterested" AI to sentence prisoners, would that make the sentences more just? What does your answer say about Shapin's paradox?
