**The question for today:** Who turns an "is" into an "ought"?

"Manufacturing" here does not mean *faking*. The warming is real. The question is how a well-established scientific finding came to be described, from about 2004, as a "crisis", and by whom.

## Why the IPCC looks the way it does

The 1987 Montreal Protocol on ozone was a success. It became the template for climate, but the template didn't fit. Ozone had a few identifiable sources (CFCs), substitutes were available, and the harm (skin cancer) was near and personal. CO₂ comes from the foundation of the world economy. As one participant put it, "Politics caught up with ozone … but climate was born in politics."

The IPCC's founding chair, Bert Bolin, saw that assessments written by a few Western scientists would not convince "people in India or Indonesia". So governments were brought *into* the assessment, approving summaries word by word. That gave the reports legitimacy. But it also let political actors fight over economic interests *as if* they were scientific disputes:

- Saudi Arabia pushed to replace "carbon dioxide" with "greenhouse gases".
- The US blocked the island nation of Kiribati's wording that sea-level rise "would" (rather than "could") threaten islands.

The first IPCC report (1990) was "lowest-common-denominator science", and every word was credible. It also contained "no exciting or surprising news" and got little press.

## The absence of crisis words

Bolin wrote in 2007: "Words such as catastrophe, apocalyptic change, threat to mankind, etc. are not found in the IPCC reports." This was deliberate. The IPCC's authority came from staying policy-neutral. In 1995 its famous finding of a "discernible human influence" was the result of careful wording on grounds of caution.

## The gap is defended

- Industry groups (the Global Climate Coalition, from 1989) and think tanks sent "slick publications" to journalists, using the playbook from tobacco and acid rain.
- A 1990 White House memo advised that the best response was "to raise the many uncertainties".
- Journalists often presented climate as a debate between two equal sides.
- Opponents used an "economic precautionary principle": any climate policy was assumed to be an economic threat unless proven otherwise. Howe describes the closed loop: "Scientific uncertainty justified greater economic precaution. Advocates of greater economic precaution consequently highlighted scientific uncertainty."
- In 1997 the US Senate voted 95–0 against any treaty that exempted developing countries. Kyoto was never submitted for ratification. Its market mechanisms treated a tonne of CO₂ saved in India as equal to one saved in the US, setting aside questions of equity and historical responsibility.

## The gap is filled

Between 2004 and 2007 "climate crisis" entered the titles of books and speeches: Mark Lynas's *High Tide*, Ross Gelbspan's *Boiling Point*, Al Gore at the 2004 Democratic convention, Bill McKibben's 2006 op-ed "Welcome to the Climate Crisis", the film *An Inconvenient Truth* (2006) and the Stern Review.

The sociologist Eric Paglia offers a mechanism:
- **Contributory experts** (working climate scientists) were bound by norms of restraint.
- **Interactional experts** (journalists, activists, politicians fluent in the science but not producing it) could say what scientists would not.

They used "iconic numbers": the Keeling Curve, the global mean temperature, the "hockey stick", 2 °C, 350 ppm. Weart adds that the turning points were mostly events and images (the 2003 European heat wave, Hurricane Katrina, a film), not new data.

## Restraint ends

In 2006 the scientist Barrie Pittock wrote in *Eos* that scientists had "consciously or unconsciously downplayed the more extreme possibilities … to avoid scaring people. However, true responsibility is to provide evidence of what must be avoided." The 2007 IPCC report and its Nobel Peace Prize (shared with Gore) mark the end of the restraint era.

The change shows in *how* the IPCC reports risk, not only in what it finds. By 2021 it was setting out "low-likelihood, high-impact" outcomes explicitly. For example, sea-level rise approaching 2 m by 2100 under very high emissions "cannot be ruled out" because ice-sheet processes are deeply uncertain. In 2007 such processes had been left out of the projections. A scientific *norm* had changed: what counts as responsible reporting of a danger you cannot yet quantify.

## Targets came from politics first

- **2 °C** began as an economist's rule of thumb (Nordhaus, 1977). It was then adopted by policy meetings (1987), the EU (1996) and the Copenhagen conference (2009).
- **1.5 °C** was pushed by small island states from 2009. The Paris Agreement (2015) wrote it in as a limit to "pursue efforts" towards and asked the IPCC to assess it. The IPCC's *Global Warming of 1.5 °C* report followed in 2018.

The science then worked out what these targets meant. The IPCC assesses targets; it does not set or endorse them.

## From construction to invisibility

By 2018 the IPCC's own report opened by calling climate change "an urgent and potentially irreversible threat". "Climate emergency" went mainstream in 2019. By 2024 UNESCO's curriculum guidance listed "scientifically accurate" and "conveys urgency" side by side as qualities of good teaching material. The crisis frame had become so normal that it was invisible.
