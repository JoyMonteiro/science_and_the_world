**The question for today:** Who turns an "is" into an "ought"?

"Manufacturing" here does not mean *faking* — the warming is real. The question this lecture asks is how a well-established scientific finding came, from about 2004 onwards, to be described as a "crisis", and by whom.

We begin with why the IPCC was designed the way it was, and why its reports avoided crisis words. We then look at how the gap between the science and the alarm was first defended and then filled by people outside the IPCC, how restraint within climate science came to an end, and where the temperature targets came from. We close in 2024, by which time the crisis frame had become so familiar that it was no longer noticed.

## Why the IPCC looks the way it does

The 1987 Montreal Protocol on ozone was a success, and it became the template for climate — but the template did not fit. Ozone depletion had a few identifiable sources (CFCs), substitutes were available, and the harm (skin cancer) was near and personal. CO₂, on the other hand, comes from the foundation of the world economy. As one participant put it, "Politics caught up with ozone … but climate was born in politics."

The IPCC's founding chair, Bert Bolin, saw that assessments written by a few Western scientists would not convince "people in India or Indonesia". Governments were therefore brought *into* the assessment, approving its summaries word by word. This gave the reports legitimacy. However, it also allowed political actors to fight over economic interests *as if* they were scientific disputes:

- Saudi Arabia pushed to replace "carbon dioxide" with "greenhouse gases".
- The US blocked the island nation of Kiribati's wording that sea-level rise "would" (rather than "could") threaten islands.

The first IPCC report (1990) was "lowest-common-denominator science", and every word in it was credible. It also contained "no exciting or surprising news", and received little press.

## The absence of crisis words

Bolin wrote in 2007: "Words such as catastrophe, apocalyptic change, threat to mankind, etc. are not found in the IPCC reports." This was deliberate, since the IPCC's authority came from staying policy-neutral. In 1995, its famous finding of a "discernible human influence" was likewise the result of careful wording on grounds of caution.

## The gap is defended

- Industry groups (the Global Climate Coalition, from 1989) and think tanks sent "slick publications" to journalists, using the playbook developed for tobacco and acid rain.
- A 1990 White House memo advised that the best response was "to raise the many uncertainties".
- Journalists often presented climate as a debate between two equal sides.
- Opponents used an "economic precautionary principle", under which any climate policy was assumed to be an economic threat unless proven otherwise. Howe describes the closed loop that resulted: "Scientific uncertainty justified greater economic precaution. Advocates of greater economic precaution consequently highlighted scientific uncertainty."
- In 1997 the US Senate voted 95–0 against any treaty that exempted developing countries, and Kyoto was never submitted for ratification. Its market mechanisms treated a tonne of CO₂ saved in India as equal to one saved in the US, setting aside questions of equity and historical responsibility.

## The gap is filled

Between 2004 and 2007, "climate crisis" entered the titles of books and speeches: Mark Lynas's *High Tide*, Ross Gelbspan's *Boiling Point*, Al Gore at the 2004 Democratic convention, Bill McKibben's 2006 op-ed "Welcome to the Climate Crisis", the film *An Inconvenient Truth* (2006) and the Stern Review.

The sociologist Eric Paglia offers a mechanism for how this happened:
- **Contributory experts** — working climate scientists — were bound by norms of restraint.
- **Interactional experts** — journalists, activists and politicians who were fluent in the science but did not produce it — could say what scientists would not.

These interactional experts made use of "iconic numbers": the Keeling Curve, the global mean temperature, the "hockey stick", 2 °C and 350 ppm. Weart adds that the turning points were mostly events and images — the 2003 European heat wave, Hurricane Katrina, a film — rather than new data.

## Restraint ends

In 2006 the scientist Barrie Pittock wrote in *Eos* that scientists had "consciously or unconsciously downplayed the more extreme possibilities … to avoid scaring people. However, true responsibility is to provide evidence of what must be avoided." The 2007 IPCC report, and the Nobel Peace Prize it shared with Gore, mark the end of the era of restraint.

The change is visible in *how* the IPCC reports risk, and not only in what it finds. By 2021 it was setting out "low-likelihood, high-impact" outcomes explicitly. For example, sea-level rise approaching 2 m by 2100 under very high emissions "cannot be ruled out", because ice-sheet processes are deeply uncertain — whereas in 2007, such processes had simply been left out of the projections. What had changed was a scientific *norm*: what counts as responsible reporting of a danger that cannot yet be quantified.

## Targets came from politics first

- **2 °C** began as an economist's rule of thumb (Nordhaus, 1977). It was then adopted by policy meetings (1987), by the EU (1996) and at the Copenhagen conference (2009).
- **1.5 °C** was pushed by the small island states from 2009 onwards. The Paris Agreement (2015) wrote it in as a limit towards which parties would "pursue efforts", and asked the IPCC to assess it. The IPCC's *Global Warming of 1.5 °C* report followed in 2018.

Only then did the science work out what these targets meant. We emphasise that the IPCC assesses targets; it does not set or endorse them.

## From construction to invisibility

By 2018, the IPCC's own report opened by calling climate change "an urgent and potentially irreversible threat". "Climate emergency" went mainstream in 2019. By 2024, UNESCO's curriculum guidance listed "scientifically accurate" and "conveys urgency" side by side as qualities of good teaching material. Thus, the crisis frame had become so normal that it was no longer visible as a frame at all.

## Nature of science: what climate adds

**The textbook statements.** The same two as in Lecture 5 — scientific knowledge is reliable *and* open to revision — together with the statement that science "affects society and cultures, and it is affected by the society and culture within which it occurs" (NSTA 2020, pp. 2–3).

**What the climate case adds.** This lecture shows the NOS statements being *used*, by people outside science, for purposes of their own. The 1990 White House memo's advice "to raise the many uncertainties" turned the tentativeness of science into a reason for delay, and the journalistic convention of two equal sides turned the openness of science into a false balance. Clough and Herman note this pattern in general: "some policy-makers and business people use uncertainty to create unreasonable doubt and inaction on public policy matters" (2017, p. 23). The crisis frame that followed was assembled by a different group — Paglia's interactional experts — who took the *is* from the science and supplied the *ought*.

What the list leaves out is precisely who speaks for science, through which channels and with what authority. Allchin and colleagues note the criticism that consensus lists "ignore the communicative aspects of science" (2014, p. 463), and the teachers in their project added the credibility of journals and news media to their inventory (p. 477). The distinction between contributory and interactional experts is the climate case's version of this feature.

**For the science classroom.** In one US survey, 85 % of participants supported teaching "both sides" of the public controversy over climate science (Clough & Herman 2017, p. 16). Clough and Herman's reply is that teaching climate "does not mean teaching 'both sides' as credible ideas, but rather accurately portraying the nature of science and the overwhelming scientific evidence" (p. 24). The other half of this lecture has a lesson for teachers as well. By 2024 UNESCO was listing "scientifically accurate" and "conveys urgency" side by side as qualities of good teaching material. A teacher who chooses crisis vocabulary is taking the step from *is* to *ought* in front of the class. That step can be defended, but it should be made visibly, so that students can tell the scientific finding from the judgement added to it. The lexicon ladder exercise above is one way to do this.

## In class: the lexicon ladder
Search for *crisis, catastrophe, emergency, threat, danger, disaster, urgent* in: (1) the 1979 Charney summary; (2) Bolin's 2007 statement; (3) the IPCC 2018 FAQ 1.1; (4) UNESCO 2024 guidance. What changes, and when? Then set it beside the *confidence ladder* from Lecture 5 ("discernible" 1995 → "unequivocal" 2021). Did the crisis words arrive when the scientific confidence rose, or at some other time?

## Questions to think about
1. Was the IPCC's restraint a virtue, a bias, or a value judgement in disguise?
2. Who should be allowed to say "this is a crisis": scientists, activists, governments? What does each gain and risk?
3. If 1.5 °C was first a political demand, does that make it less scientific? Less legitimate?
